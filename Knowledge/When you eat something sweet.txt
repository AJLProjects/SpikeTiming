It's Not a "Flood" of the Whole Brain, It's Targeted Release by Neurons:
You're right, it's neurons doing this, not a passive flood.
Dopamine release in response to rewarding stimuli like sweet tastes is primarily concentrated in specific brain circuits, most notably the mesolimbic pathway.
This pathway involves dopamine-producing neurons originating in the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) located in the midbrain.
These VTA neurons project their axons to and release dopamine in target areas, especially the Nucleus Accumbens (NAc), which is a key part of the brain's reward center. Other areas like the amygdala (emotions) and prefrontal cortex (decision-making, attention) also receive dopamine from the VTA.
So, while the effects can influence overall feelings and behaviour, the initial dopamine release is a targeted neurochemical event within specific pathways, not a brain-wide drenching.
How the Reward Dopamine Cascade Works:
Here's a simplified step-by-step breakdown of the cascade initiated by eating something sweet:
Step 1: Sensory Input: Taste receptors on your tongue detect the sweet molecules. This generates signals that travel through nerves to your brainstem.
Step 2: Signal Relay to VTA: Signals from the brainstem (and potentially higher processing centers that recognize the food as desirable) reach the VTA.
Step 3: VTA Neuron Activation: The incoming signals excite specific dopamine-producing neurons in the VTA. These neurons begin to fire action potentials more frequently.
Step 4: Dopamine Release in NAc (and other areas): The action potentials travel down the axons of the VTA neurons to their terminals in the Nucleus Accumbens (and PFC, amygdala, etc.). This triggers the release of dopamine molecules into the synaptic cleft (the tiny gap between the VTA neuron terminal and the NAc neuron).
Step 5: Dopamine Binding in NAc: Released dopamine crosses the synapse and binds to dopamine receptors (both D1-like excitatory and D2-like inhibitory types) on the surface of neurons in the NAc.
Step 6: Postsynaptic Effects & Signaling:
Binding to D1 receptors tends to excite the NAc neurons, making them more likely to fire. This is linked to the motivational "wanting" and reinforcing aspects.
Binding to D2 receptors tends to inhibit NAc neurons (or modulate dopamine release itself via autoreceptors), helping to fine-tune the response.
The overall effect of this dopamine surge in the NAc is a complex modulation of its activity, strongly associated with processing reward, pleasure, motivation, and reinforcement learning.
Step 7: Downstream Influence: The change in activity within the NAc influences other brain regions it connects to:
Prefrontal Cortex: Affects decision-making, planning, and focusing attention on the rewarding stimulus and associated cues. It helps link the action (eating sweet food) with the outcome (pleasure).
Amygdala: Processes the emotional valence (the positive feeling) associated with the sweet taste.
Hippocampus: Helps form memories of the rewarding experience (what it was, where you got it).
Basal Ganglia (motor loops): Reinforces the motor patterns that led to obtaining the reward (e.g., reaching for the food, chewing).
Step 8: Behavioural Outcome: This entire cascade results in the feeling of pleasure/reward, increased motivation to consume more of the sweet food (or seek it out again later), and learning that this particular food/action is desirable. It reinforces the behaviour.
